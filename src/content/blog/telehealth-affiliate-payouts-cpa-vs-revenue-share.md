Every telehealth offer has a payout structure, and the structure decides who carries the risk. Get it wrong and one side quietly loses money for months. Here is how the three common models actually work, in plain English.

## CPA: a fixed amount per action

CPA stands for cost per action. The brand defines one event, almost always a paid first order, and pays the partner a fixed amount every time it fires. A GLP-1 offer might pay a few hundred dollars per first paid month. An ED offer might pay less because the first order is smaller.

**What the partner likes:** it is simple to model. If a partner's cost per sale is under the payout, they are profitable today, not next quarter.

**What the brand likes:** the cost per new patient is capped and known before launch. Finance can plan.

**Where it breaks:** the brand carries all retention risk. If patients cancel after one month, the brand paid a full CPA for a customer who never paid back the acquisition cost. That is why serious CPA offers come with quality rules: real traffic sources, approved claims, and monitoring of refund and cancellation rates by partner.

## Revenue share: a percentage over time

Under revenue share, the partner earns a percentage of what the patient actually pays, month after month. If the patient stays six months, the partner gets paid six times.

**What the partner likes:** upside. A partner who sends patients who stick around earns far more than a flat CPA would pay.

**What the brand likes:** aligned incentives. Nobody gets paid for a patient who cancels in week two, so partners are rewarded for quality, not just volume.

**Where it breaks:** cash flow and trust. The partner is out of pocket for ad spend today and gets paid slowly. And revenue share only works if tracking covers rebills, refunds, and cancellations accurately. If the brand cannot show the partner an auditable ledger of renewals, the relationship falls apart.

## Hybrid: a smaller CPA plus a share

The hybrid model pays a reduced fixed amount on the first order plus a percentage of later payments. It splits the difference: partners get enough cash to keep buying media, brands get partners who care about retention.

Hybrid structures are the most common landing spot for offers that started on CPA and matured. Once both sides have three or four months of retention data, the conversation moves from "what is the payout" to "how do we both make more."

## Which one should you pick?

| Situation | Usual structure |
| --- | --- |
| New offer, unproven retention | CPA |
| Proven retention, partner wants upside | Hybrid |
| Long-term partner, full tracking on rebills | Revenue share |
| Partner running paid media at scale | CPA or hybrid (cash flow matters) |
| Partner running owned audiences (email, SMS, community) | Revenue share often wins |

The honest answer for most new telehealth offers is CPA first. It is the only structure a media buyer can model without trusting your retention numbers on faith.

## What has to be true for any of these to work

- **Terms locked before launch.** The payout, the qualifying event, the cap, and what disqualifies a sale, all agreed in writing before the first click.
- **One source of truth for conversions.** Usually a server-to-server postback into a tracking platform both sides can see. Screenshots from a dashboard are not a ledger.
- **A clear definition of "approved."** Refunds, chargebacks, duplicate accounts, and fraud all need a rule. Partners should know how long approval takes and what gets reversed.
- **Payment timing in writing.** Net 15, net 30, weekly for proven partners. Whatever it is, it should not be a surprise.
